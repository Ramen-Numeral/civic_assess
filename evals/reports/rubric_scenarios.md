### Response

## Answer

In 2023, after weeks of intense negotiations, President Biden and House Speaker McCarthy reached an agreement known as the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023, which suspended the debt ceiling until January 1, 2025. This deal included limits on discretionary government spending and other provisions, and was passed by Congress and signed into law on June 3, 2023, thereby averting a US default. The debt ceiling had been reached on January 19, 2023, with Treasury Secretary Yellen estimating the US would run out of money by June 5, 2023, which set a critical deadline for the negotiations. <a href="https://www.aacom.org/docs/default-source/advocacy/public-statements/nj_2023_debt_limit_tracker_060623.pdf?sfvrsn=b41d09ff_3">[1]</a><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_United_States_debt-ceiling_crisis">[2]</a><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_debt_ceiling">[3]</a><a href="https://budget.house.gov/press-release/a-nation-in-crisis-democrat-spending-accelerates-debt-ceiling-deadline">[4]</a>

Democrats, led by President Biden and Senate Majority Leader Schumer, publicly maintained that raising the debt ceiling was non-negotiable and separate from budget negotiations, emphasizing the need to avoid default. However, behind the scenes, they took steps to enable an 11th hour deal to raise or suspend the debt limit. Republicans, led by House Speaker McCarthy and negotiators like Reps. McHenry and Graves, demanded significant federal spending cuts and a change in the trajectory of government spending as conditions for agreeing to raise the debt ceiling. Some House Republicans, including the House Freedom Caucus, resisted raising the debt ceiling without spending cuts, reflecting internal party divisions and a cultural shift toward reducing federal spending. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_United_States_debt-ceiling_crisis">[2]</a><a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2023/05/02/debt-ceiling-democrats-deal.html">[5]</a><a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2023/05/20/dems-debt-limit-biden-00097925">[6]</a><a href="https://ncnewsline.com/2023/05/25/after-years-of-bipartisan-spending-boosts-u-s-house-gop-wont-lift-debt-ceiling-without-cuts">[7]</a>

### Response

## Answer

The Supreme Court's recent decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard and UNC ruled that the universities' race-conscious admissions programs violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, effectively ending affirmative action in college admissions. The Court found that Harvard and UNC's policies lacked sufficiently focused and measurable objectives to justify the use of race and failed strict scrutiny, overruling the prior precedent set by Grutter v. Bollinger. Chief Justice Roberts emphasized that admissions must treat applicants as individuals based on their experiences, not race, allowing race to be discussed only in personal essays but not as a factor in admissions decisions themselves. <a href="https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2023/08/us-supreme-court-ends-affirmative-action-in-higher-education--an-overview-and-practical-next-steps">[1]</a><a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10893">[2]</a><a href="https://www.naceweb.org/public-policy-and-legal/legal-issues/us-supreme-court-strikes-down-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions-how-the-decision-impacts-institutions-of-higher-education">[3]</a><a href="https://www.scotusblog.com/2023/06/supreme-court-strikes-down-affirmative-action-programs-in-college-admissions">[4]</a>

The Court did not address the use of race in admissions at military academies, leaving that issue open for future consideration. Justice Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, arguing that the decision rolled back decades of precedent and progress by imposing a superficial colorblindness in a segregated society. <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10893">[2]</a><a href="https://www.scotusblog.com/2023/06/supreme-court-strikes-down-affirmative-action-programs-in-college-admissions">[4]</a>

## Answer

The current debate on U.S. immigration policy revolves around balancing economic benefits, border security, and humanitarian concerns amid significant political divides. Advocates highlight immigration's role in sustaining economic growth and addressing demographic challenges, while opponents emphasize border enforcement and concerns about unauthorized migration. This divide reflects differing priorities between support for orderly legal immigration and stricter border control. Recent legislative proposals, such as the Trump-endorsed RAISE Act, seek to reduce family-based immigration and introduce a points-based system, illustrating ongoing debates over immigration levels and criteria. Immigration advocacy groups call for modernizing legal immigration policies to align with economic needs and public support, emphasizing that strong border control is a prerequisite for broader acceptance. Immigrants themselves hold diverse views, with many supporting pathways to citizenship and access to benefits, though opinions vary by status and political affiliation, underscoring the complexity within affected communities. <a href="https://www.migrationpolicy.org/commentary/immigration-debate-america-needs-and-not-having">[1]</a><a href="https://www.cfr.org/backgrounders/us-immigration-debate-0">[2]</a><a href="https://about.bgov.com/insights/federal-policy/a-guide-to-immigration-policy-changes-in-2025">[3]</a><a href="https://www.migrationpolicy.org/commentary/raise-act-dramatic-change-family-immigration-less-so-employment-based-system">[4]</a><a href="https://www.kff.org/racial-equity-and-health-policy/political-preferences-and-views-on-us-immigration-policy-among-immigrants-in-the-us">[5]</a>
